Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital

The largest public hospital in southern Malawi.

Overview

A hospital for the entire south of the country.

The Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH) is located in Blantyre, Malawi's commercial hub. It opened in 1958 and is today the largest state central hospital in the country. With around 1,350 beds and a wide range of specialist departments, it is also the highest level of care for the whole of southern Malawi.

When smaller district hospitals, health centres or private facilities cannot help any further, patients are referred here. That makes it the last and often only place to turn to for millions of people in the region – so the wards are always packed and the daily flow of patients is enormous.

At the same time, the QECH is a teaching hospital: this is where future doctors are trained, and it is affiliated with the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences. And because it is a public hospital, people are treated even if they cannot pay. Meanwhile the team has worked under chronic underfunding for years – there is a lack of equipment, consumables and medication.

Areas & workflow

The emergency department

Every day, dozens of patients come to the emergency department. It is divided into several areas – Triage, Priority, Trauma, Resuscitation and Short Stay. On top of that there is a room for minor surgical procedures and a gynaecological emergency room. Children even have a completely separate emergency department of their own.

Everything starts in triage: the team assesses how urgently someone needs help, and then they move on to the right area. Priority – as in Germany – is for people with all kinds of conditions. Short Stay is for anyone who needs to be monitored overnight, and the trauma room is for stable patients after accidents, for example with open wounds.

I spend most of my time in Resuscitation. This is where the critically ill arrive: with brain haemorrhages, often caused by untreated high blood pressure, after car accidents or physical assaults, with severe tuberculosis and much more. Many are already only semi-conscious on arrival and would get a resuscitation room straight away in Germany. Here there is barely space for that – often five or more critically ill patients share one room.

For the two resuscitation rooms there is one consultant, two junior doctors and the nursing staff between them. There is oxygen and a few medications, plus a single monitor for everyone. It can happen that someone stops breathing without anyone noticing straight away. When the rooms are full – which is often – patients sit or lie in the corridor. For imaging there is X-ray and CT – but they serve the entire hospital, so it usually takes a long time before the scans are done.
